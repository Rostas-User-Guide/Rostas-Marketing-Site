How to Set Up a Church Volunteer Roster

Most church rosters weren't designed. They grew: a list on the noticeboard, a spreadsheet someone started years ago, a few people who always say yes. Here's how to set one up on purpose, so Sunday runs smoothly and the same handful of people aren't carrying it.

1. List the roles Sunday actually needs

Start with what has to happen for a service to run, not with the people you have. Walk through a Sunday from unlocking the doors to washing the last cup. Most churches end up with something like this:

AreaTypical roles
WelcomeDoor greeter, welcome desk, ushers
ServiceReader, prayers, communion, offering
Music and techMusicians, sound desk, slides, livestream
Children and youthKids' church leaders and helpers, crèche
HospitalityMorning tea, setting up, packing down

Then cut. A role nobody has been rostered against for three months is noise. Fold it into another role or retire it. A shorter list is easier to fill and easier to read.

2. Decide how often each role comes round

Not every role should rotate at the same speed. A good rule of thumb:

  • Skilled roles (sound desk, music, slides) have fewer people who can do them, so each person comes round more often. Keep an eye on them: these are the people most likely to burn out.
  • Open roles (welcome, morning tea, offering) can rotate widely. Once a month per person is plenty, and spreading them out is a gentle way to involve newer people.
  • Children's ministry usually works best with small, stable teams that rotate together, so children see familiar faces. Follow your church's safeguarding policy for who can be rostered here, and make sure the roster can't accidentally put someone in this role who hasn't completed the checks.

Write the rule down for each role, for example "no more than once every three weeks". It turns "is it fair?" from a feeling into something you can check.

3. Ask for availability before you assign anyone

The biggest time-saver in rostering is asking first. One short message, sent before you build the roster, turns the job from guessing and re-asking into simple matching:

"Hi Sam, I'm putting together the roster for March and April. Which of these Sundays can't you do? 1, 8, 15, 22, 29 March, 5, 12, 19, 26 April. Reply with the dates you're away and I'll roster around them."

Ask which dates people can't do rather than which they can. Most people are free most weeks, so the list they send back is shorter and more accurate. While you're at it, ask which roles they enjoy and which they'd rather avoid. People rostered to things they like turn up.

4. Choose a roster length

Six to eight weeks ahead is the sweet spot for most churches. Far enough out that people can plan around it; close enough that availability doesn't go stale. Many churches line the roster up with the church calendar (a term, or Advent to Easter) so it's easy to remember when the next one is due.

5. Fill it in: primary and backup together

For each date and role, put in a volunteer and a backup. It takes an extra minute now and saves you a Saturday-night phone tree later. If you're working from a spreadsheet, the free volunteer roster template has a column for exactly this.

As you fill it in, keep a running count of how many times each person is on. If the same names keep appearing, stop and look for who else could do it before you finish.

6. Publish it once, then remind people

Send the whole roster to everyone at once, in the format people will actually look at: email, a printout on the noticeboard, or a shared link. Then send each person a reminder a few days before their date, and a short one the day before. Reminders aren't nagging; they're what stops "oh, was that this week?"

Not sure what to say? There are copy-and-paste wordings in the volunteer reminder templates guide.

7. Plan for the week someone can't make it

Someone always can't make it. Make it easy for them to tell you early, and have a plan for finding a replacement that doesn't depend on a group text. The no-show and replacement guide walks through it step by step.

8. Review it every season

At the end of each roster, take ten minutes to look back. Who was on the most? Which roles were hard to fill? Who joined the church recently and hasn't been asked yet? A small adjustment each season keeps the roster fair, and keeps it from drifting back to the same few people. The guide to preventing volunteer burnout has more on spotting the warning signs.

A roster is a promise that the load is shared. When people can see it's fair, they're far more willing to say yes, and far more likely to keep saying yes.
