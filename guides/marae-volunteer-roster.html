How to Roster Volunteers for a Marae Hui or Tangihanga

A marae runs on the aroha and mahi of its people. The ringawera in the kitchen, the whānau making up beds in the wharenui, the people on the gate and the ones still washing dishes at midnight. When the work is shared well, manuhiri are cared for and nobody is worn out. When it isn't, the same few aunties carry every hui.

This guide is about the practical side: who is doing which job, when, and how to make sure the load is shared. It isn't about tikanga. Kawa differs from marae to marae, and between hapū and iwi, and decisions about the paepae, karanga and how things are done belong with your kaumātua and marae committee. A good roster supports those decisions; it never makes them.

1. List the jobs for a typical hui

Walk through a hui from the day before to the last pack-down and write down every job. Most marae end up with something like this:

AreaTypical jobs
WharekaiHead cook, ringawera, hāngi or umu team, setting and waiting on tables, dishes, tea and coffee, kai for the workers
Kai and suppliesKitchen planner, buying and collecting kai, receiving koha of food, stocktake afterwards
Wharenui and beddingSetting out mattresses, linen and pillows, airing and laundry afterwards, keeping the wharenui tidy
Grounds and facilitiesOpening up, parking, firewood, rubbish and recycling, cleaning the wharepaku and showers, locking up
Paepae supportWho is available as kaikaranga, kaikōrero and kaiwaiata on each day, as decided by your kaumātua
Welcome and safetyGate or entrance, first aid, a contact for manuhiri, wardens for large hui

For the paepae, the roster is a list of who is available, not a decision about who stands. Many marae are short of speakers and callers, and knowing in advance that only one kaikōrero is home that weekend gives your kaumātua time to call on whanaunga from further away.

2. Plan for two kinds of hui

A marae roster has to cope with two very different situations.

  • Planned hui: wānanga, reunions, birthdays, weddings, school or group stays, Waitangi Day and other booked events. You know the dates weeks ahead, so you can ask for availability, build the roster and send reminders like any other volunteer roster.
  • Tangihanga: no notice, and usually three days or more with people sleeping on the marae. There's no time to ask for availability first, so the work has to be ready before it's needed.

For tangihanga, keep a standing call-out list for each job: the people willing to be rung for the kitchen, for bedding, for the gate. When a tangi comes, the kitchen planner works down the list rather than starting from scratch. Split each day into shifts (an early breakfast crew, lunch, dinner, and a late crew for dishes and the next morning's preparation) so nobody is in the kitchen from dawn to midnight three days in a row.

Leave the whānau pani off the roster entirely. Their place is with their loved one, and caring for them is the reason everyone else is in the kitchen.

3. Share the work across whānau, not just individuals

Marae work is often organised by whānau, or by hapū on a shared marae. That's a strength: people work well beside their own, and a whānau that turns up together makes one trip. A few habits keep it fair:

  • Count turns per whānau as well as per person, so a big whānau isn't asked every time and a small one isn't forgotten.
  • Rotate the heavy jobs. Dishes, the late shift and cleaning the wharepaku shouldn't always fall to the same whānau.
  • Keep the head cook role in a few safe pairs of hands, but roster a younger cook alongside them, so the knowledge is passed on before it's needed.
  • Write it down. A simple tally of who did what at each hui stops the quiet resentment that builds when some whānau always show up and others never do.

Our guide to preventing volunteer burnout has more on keeping the load even.

4. Bring in whanaunga who live away

Many marae have more whānau living in the cities, or across the Tasman, than at home. They often want to help and don't know when or how. Ask them directly, and give them jobs that fit around travel: a Saturday kitchen shift during a weekend hui, buying and dropping off kai on the way in, or the big clean after a tangi. A short message well before a booked hui ("we need four for dishes on the Saturday night, can you come?") gets far more yeses than a general call on the day.

Rangatahi are the next generation of ringawera and paepae. Rostering them alongside experienced people, rather than leaving them to find their own way in, is how marae keep their skills alive.

5. Look after the people doing the mahi

Ringawera are the people you often don't see, and they're the ones most likely to be worn out. Make sure the roster includes:

  • Breaks and meals for the workers, not just for manuhiri.
  • A clear finish time for each shift, with someone named to take over.
  • A thank-you afterwards, to every whānau who helped. It's the best start to rostering the next hui.

6. A quick word on food safety

According to MPI, food prepared and served on a marae for customary events such as tangihanga is outside the Food Act 2014, so you don't need to register a food safety plan for those. Selling food is different: a fundraiser or catering for paying groups may need registration. MPI's "My Food Rules" tool tells you what applies, and its marae food safety guide, Te Kai Manawa Ora, covers safe practice in a marae kitchen. It's worth having at least one person with food safety training on every kitchen shift.

Start with the marae committee and kaumātua. Before you change how the marae is rostered, talk it through with the people who look after the marae and its kawa. They'll know which jobs belong to which whānau, and which ones must never be put on a list.

For the hui you can plan

Rostas asks your whānau for availability, builds the roster within each person's limits ("no more than one weekend a month") and the jobs they've said they'll do, and rosters people in the same household on the same day, so a whānau makes one trip. People answer in the Rostas app or by text, reminders go out before each hui, and when someone can't make it Rostas asks the next person who does that job, one at a time. Who stands on the paepae stays with your kaumātua.
