How to Prevent Volunteer Burnout

Volunteers rarely quit over one bad Sunday. They quit after months of being the person who always says yes. The good news: most burnout starts on the roster, which means the roster is where you can stop it.

How burnout happens on a roster

It follows the same pattern almost everywhere. A slot needs filling at short notice. The coordinator asks the person most likely to say yes. They say yes. Next time, the coordinator asks them first again, because it worked. Nobody decides to overload anyone; the roster just quietly drifts towards the most reliable people until they've had enough.

By the time someone says "I need a break", they've usually been carrying too much for a long while. The aim is to catch it months earlier.

Warning signs to watch for

  • The same names on every list. If you can guess who's on next week without looking, the load isn't shared.
  • Slower replies. Someone who used to answer within the hour now takes days, or stops replying to availability requests at all.
  • "I can do it if nobody else can." A yes with a condition attached is often a no being polite.
  • More last-minute cancellations from usually reliable people. Tired people's plans give way first.
  • Someone doing two roles on the same day because it was easier than finding a second person.

Five ways to share the load fairly

  1. Set a limit for each role, and write it down. "No one does sound desk more than every second week." "Morning tea: once a month each." A written limit protects your keenest people from their own generosity, and gives you a reason to look further down the list.
  2. Count, don't guess. Keep a tally of how often each person has been rostered this season, including fill-ins. Fill-ins are where overload hides: they don't show on the original roster, but they're felt just the same.
  3. Ask about preferences, then honour them. Someone who loves welcoming people may dread running the slides. People rostered to roles they enjoy last far longer. Ask once a year and update the list.
  4. Grow the pool on purpose. Most churches and clubs have people who'd help if asked personally. Pair a newcomer with an experienced volunteer for their first couple of times, so the skilled roles don't depend on two people forever.
  5. Make saying no easy and guilt-free. If it's awkward to decline, people say yes and resent it, or say yes and don't come. A simple "can't make it" button or reply, with no explanation needed, keeps the roster honest.

Rest is part of the roster

Build breaks in, rather than waiting for people to ask. Some groups give everyone a month off each year; others rotate whole teams so each has a rest season. Holidays and busy times of year (exams, harvest, the end of the year) are good moments to roster lighter. A planned break is far better than losing someone completely.

Say thank you, specifically

A general "thanks to all our volunteers" is nice. A message that says "thanks for doing sound at short notice on Sunday, it made a real difference" is remembered. It takes thirty seconds, and it's one of the cheapest ways to keep good people. There are ready-made wordings in the volunteer reminder templates, including a thank-you.

Remember the coordinator too. The person most at risk of burnout is often the one holding the roster together. If every cancellation lands on one person's phone at 9pm on a Saturday, that's a burnout risk in itself. A clear replacement plan (see the no-show guide) protects the coordinator as much as the volunteers.

Fairness built into every roster

In Rostas you set how often each role can come round, and Rostas builds the roster around those limits and each volunteer's preferences and availability. When someone can't make it, it asks the next-best person automatically, so replacements don't always land on the same few, or on you.
