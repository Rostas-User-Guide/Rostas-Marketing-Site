How to Recruit Volunteers

Most people who could help have simply never been asked, or were asked in a way that was easy to ignore. Recruiting is less about finding the right advert and more about asking the right person, clearly, for something small.

Where new volunteers actually come from

Notices in the newsletter and announcements from the front bring in a few people. Personal asks bring in far more. Someone who hears "I think you'd be great on the welcome team, would you try it once?" from a person they know is much more likely to say yes than someone reading a general call for help.

Good places to look:

  • People who already turn up. Regular attenders, parents at the sideline, the person who always stays to stack chairs. They know the place and already care about it.
  • Friends of your current volunteers. Ask each volunteer if there's one person they'd enjoy serving with. People who come in pairs tend to stay.
  • Newcomers, gently. A small, low-pressure job helps new people feel they belong. Don't wait a year before asking.
  • People who have stepped back. Someone who stopped during a busy season may be ready again, especially for a lighter role.

How to ask so people say yes

  1. Ask one person at a time. A group appeal lets everyone assume someone else will answer. A direct question gets a direct answer.
  2. Be specific about the job. "Help with morning tea" is vague. "Set out cups and the urn at 9:30, and clear up by 11:15" is something people can picture and say yes to.
  3. Say how often. The biggest fear is being signed up forever. "About once a month, and you can say no to any date" removes it.
  4. Offer a trial. "Come and try it once alongside Sue" is a much smaller step than joining a team.
  5. Say why you thought of them. "You're great at making people feel at home" is honest, and it tells them the ask isn't random.
  6. Make no an easy answer. If saying no is awkward, some people will say yes and quietly not turn up. A relaxed "no worries if not" keeps the door open for next time.

Make the first few weeks easy

The weeks after someone says yes decide whether they stay. A few things make a big difference:

  • Pair them with someone experienced for their first two or three times, so they're never left wondering what to do.
  • Give them a short how-to. A half-page note for each role (when to arrive, where things are kept, who to ask) saves a lot of nervous questions.
  • Roster them soon. Someone who signs up in March and isn't rostered until June has often lost interest by then.
  • Don't overload them. Keen new volunteers are easy to over-roster. Start with the frequency you promised and let them ask for more.
  • Check in after a month. "How's it going? Is this the right role for you?" catches problems while they're small.

Keep the pool healthy

Recruiting isn't a once-a-year drive. Volunteers move, get busy, change seasons of life. A good habit is to ask two or three new people each term, so the pool grows a little faster than it shrinks. Keeping each person's load fair matters just as much: our guide to preventing volunteer burnout covers how to stop new recruits wearing out as fast as the old ones.

Keep track as you go. Write down each new volunteer's roles, how often they're happy to serve, and any dates they can't do, as soon as they say yes. If you're starting a roster from scratch, the church roster set-up guide walks through it.
