How to Roster a Working Bee or One-Off Event

A working bee, a gala day, a fundraising dinner: one day, lots of jobs, and a lot of goodwill that's easy to waste. A simple roster turns "come along if you can" into a day where every job has someone and nobody stands around wondering what to do.

1. List every job before you ask anyone

Walk through the day in your head, from setting up to locking the gate, and write down every job. For a working bee that might be weeding, gutters, painting, the trailer run to the tip, and morning tea. For an event, think about parking, the gate or door, each stall, the kitchen, first aid, and pack-down.

For each job, note:

  • How many people it needs.
  • Any skills or gear: a driver's licence and tow bar, a food handling certificate, a ladder, a ute.
  • Who's in charge of it on the day, so helpers know who to ask.

2. Break the day into shifts

Few people can give a whole day, but many can give two hours. Splitting a long day into shifts of two to three hours usually doubles the number of people who say yes. Overlap shifts by fifteen minutes so jobs are handed over rather than abandoned.

Don't forget the bookends. Set-up and pack-down are the jobs everyone forgets, and they usually fall on the organiser. Roster them like any other job, ideally with fresh people rather than those who've worked all day.

3. Ask for names, not just numbers

"We need lots of help on Saturday" gets a few vague maybes. A sign-up list with named slots ("Gate, 9 to 11, two people") gets commitments. Share the list two or three weeks out, then go through it and ask people personally for the gaps. Our guide to recruiting volunteers has tips on asking so people say yes.

Ask each helper for a mobile number. On the day you'll want to reach them quickly, and a reminder by text is far more likely to be read than an email.

4. Remind people twice

One-off events are easier to forget than a weekly roster, because there's no habit behind them. Send a reminder a few days before, with the time, place, what to bring and who to report to, and another the day before. There are copy-and-paste wordings in the volunteer reminder templates.

5. Have a plan for no-shows

Someone will be sick, and someone will forget. Keep a short list of people who said "call me if you're stuck", and decide which jobs can drop if you're short (the second painter) and which can't (first aid, the barbecue). The no-show and replacement guide covers this in more detail.

6. On the day

  • Have a check-in point with a printed copy of the roster, so people know where to go when they arrive.
  • Give each job leader their list of names and phone numbers.
  • Feed people. Morning tea or a sausage at the end is a small cost and a big thank-you.

Afterwards, write it down. Note which jobs needed more or fewer people than you planned, and who was great at what. Next year's organiser (possibly you) will be grateful, and a thank-you message to everyone who came is the best start to recruiting for the next one.

For the rosters that come round every week

Working bees are one day. Sunday services, morning teas and music teams come round every week, and that's where Rostas helps most. It asks for availability, builds the roster within each person's limits and preferences, sends reminders, and finds replacements one person at a time when someone can't make it.
